When I was becoming a team lead, many people gave me similar advice.

Managers are supposed to delegate. Drifting away from the work itself is inevitable. The skills you used to be good at, the knowledge, the execution, will decay, and there's nothing you can do about it. You have to accept that. And because of all this, managers should be chosen for their loyalty to the organization rather than their craft.

A few people gave me slightly different advice. One said they normally delegate, but try to keep the capacity to dive into the low-level when something feels off with what their team is doing. Another said they started with micromanaging and gradually expanded delegation as trust grew. Another said when they felt their hands-on skills slipping, they'd carve out time to study, even if it meant adding hours to their week.

All of it made sense. I followed that advice for a while. I delegated, I aligned, I sat in meetings, I approved decisions, I stayed busy. Then at some point, two things hit me at once.

First. I realized that a lot of what I was doing, I wasn't doing because I was the best person for it. I was doing it because I happened to be the person who could be held accountable. I went back to first principles and asked: is my team's capacity actually being fully tapped? It didn't seem like it. I have real skills as an ML research scientist. Is it justifiable for the team to keep operating in a state where those skills go unused?

Second. My teammates were starting to push past their own domains and create end-to-end impact. Backend engineers were touching the frontend. The PM was prototyping things directly. The ML engineer was getting deep into product decisions. And there I was, off to the side, doing the work of looking down from above. So what is a manager actually for?

I spent a long time trying to answer those two questions. Then I came across an essay by Elena Verna.

## When IC work became the new career peak

Elena Verna recently wrote a piece called ["IC work is the new career flex"](https://www.elenaverna.com/p/ic-work-is-the-new-career-flex). Her core argument: becoming a manager used to be the peak of a career, but AI is now reshaping that structure. She introduces the concept of the "High-Impact Individual Contributor" (HI-C). Someone with no direct reports who can take a project end-to-end, work that used to require an entire team. She's living it herself at Lovable.

I agreed with almost everything in it. But Elena's piece ends with "and so the management layer shrinks." From where I sit, leading a team, the real question started exactly where her piece ended. If the management layer shrinks, what does the manager who remains actually do?

## Expansion happens in both directions

Here's the picture I'm seeing. The shift AI is creating doesn't move in just one direction.

ICs are expanding into managing. Team members can hold onto the depth of their own domain while borrowing depth in adjacent domains through AI. They're no longer locked into a single discipline; they can have influence across many. As that happens, much of what used to be "connecting across domains," historically a manager's job, gets absorbed naturally by the team members themselves. A structure where people manage each other becomes possible.

Managers are expanding into IC work. The places where managers used to spend most of their time, like managing complex context, mediating between stakeholders, passing information up and down, are exactly the areas AI can now assist with. And when the purpose, the criteria, and the "why" are clearly defined, execution itself gets faster. The door has opened for managers to get their hands back on the actual work.

This is the piece I want to add to Elena's argument. I deeply agree that managers can become HI-Cs. And at the same time, ICs need to become managers to each other.

In a small team, where these two directions meet, I believe you can have a structure where everyone is an IC and also a manager to each other. I lead a 7-person cross-functional product team, with backend, frontend, ML, design, and PM all in one team, and I believe we can be that kind of team.

But then the real question comes back. If everyone is an IC and everyone is managing each other, why does a separate leader role need to exist?

Here's my answer. There's one thing that doesn't disappear: setting the standards.

What counts as success and failure for our team? What should we be focusing on right now? When the reasons behind different decisions conflict with each other, what do we choose and why? Why are we doing the work we're doing?

These questions need answers for a team to stay aligned. For team members to move fast in their own domains, expand into adjacent ones, and manage each other, all of that motion has to point in the same direction, and that direction has to be defined first. AI accelerates output dramatically when the purpose, the criteria, and the reasoning are clearly given. When those are missing or vague, faster execution just takes the team to the wrong place, faster.

Which is why, in the age of AI, setting the standards doesn't become less important. It becomes more important.

## Standards can only be set by someone who actually understands the domain

This brings us back to the conventional wisdom from the start. "Managers should delegate." "Skill decay is inevitable." I'm not saying those statements are false. Back when a manager's job was coordination, they were roughly correct. Domain fluency was secondary for a coordinator.

But when a leader's job shifts to setting standards, the picture changes. Standards can only be set by someone who genuinely understands the domain.

Here's an example. Our team is building an agentic system. Before we commit resources, we have to answer questions like: How should the memory architecture be designed, and how does that affect the user experience? How do we weigh cost, latency, and robustness against each other? Is this even the right thing to be working on right now? If I have the capacity to understand these things deeply but instead, due to lack of time, hand off the judgment to someone else while still half-understanding it myself, is that really the maximum output the team can produce? I don't think so.

That's why I try not to let my hands-on instincts go. Especially in the parts of the product that touch users directly, whether it's code, features, or the user's voice, I need to understand them firsthand for the standards to come from a real place.

## When the team doesn't keep me behind my title

The Research Scientist on our team asked me to review the design and implementation of an agentic system. He said: "I'd really like you to make this decision with deep understanding." So I read the papers, read the code, and got involved in the design directly. I think he asked because he wasn't seeing me as just a manager. He was seeing me as a Research Scientist. I'm grateful for that.

The PM on our team pushed me to participate in user research directly, to show up at product events in person. He said the kind of feel for the product you get from being on the ground is on a different level. He was right. What you see from your desk through data and what you feel face-to-face with users leave completely different impressions. I'm grateful for that.

The engineers on our team pointed out that my understanding of concepts like Kafka and message brokers wasn't deep enough. And then they walked me through, step by step, what exactly I needed to know to be able to make good decisions. I'm grateful for that.

These three moments have done more to make me a real leader than any theory of management I've held in my head. The team didn't keep me locked behind my title. That's the actual mechanism through which I've been able to maintain my instincts.

## Don't let me off the hook just because I'm a manager

Honestly, the title of manager comes with a kind of comfort. "I'm a manager, so of course I have to spend most of my time on communication." "This is a big org, inefficiency is just part of the deal." "That's just what a manager's job looks like." These little compromises show up every day. Stepping away from domain fluency in the name of delegation. Getting let off the hook from challenges in the name of management. Accepting inefficiency in the name of organizational structure. They're all the same kind of compromise. I don't want to make those compromises.

So here's what I want to ask of the team going forward. Don't see me as someone who only manages. See me as someone who can contribute to the product in any way that's needed, someone who wants to, someone who will.

To do that, ask me the kinds of questions you'd ask a fellow IC, not the kinds of questions you'd ask a manager. "Is that really the right thing to be prioritizing right now?" "Do you actually understand the reasoning behind this decision?" "Have you thought through how a user would experience this?" "Can you explain why this part of the design is built the way it is?" These kinds of questions. Don't exempt me from them just because I'm a manager.
